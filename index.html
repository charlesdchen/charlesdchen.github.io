Post-doctoral research

Boston, Massachusetts | 2023-2026

After graduate school I worked on characterizing the extracerebral off-target signal (OTS) of 18F-MK-6240 tau PET. 18F-MK-6240 tau PET is a powerful tool for measuring Alzheimer disease (AD) tauopathy, but its extracerebral OTS can spill into the brain parenchyma and create the appearance of AD tauopathy. But when I developed a method to characterize this extracerebral OTS at a more granular level, namely by looking across different cortical depths and folding patterns instead of averaging 18F-MK-6240 uptake across large brain regions, I found that extracerebral OTS spill-in tends to create higher 18F-MK-6240 uptake values at the pial surfaces of gyri, while true AD tauopathy tends to create higher 18F-MK-6240 uptake values at the midthickness surfaces of gyri. Additionally, sulci tend not to be impacted by extracerebral OTS spill-in, but can be affected by AD tauopathy.

Graduate research

St. Louis, Missouri | 2018-2023

In graduate school I worked on studying how well PET imaging could be used to diagnose and monitor Alzheimer disease (AD). One way to do this is to see how well antemortem PET imaging compares with postmortem neuropathology. To faciliate these sorts of studies, I co-founded the Pathology And Imaging Neuroinflammation and Tau (PAINT) committee to coordinate tissue transfers between the Knight ADRC Imaging and Neuropathology Cores, and joined the Dominantly Inherited Alzheimer Network (DIAN) to study autosomal dominant AD, which is thought to be a relatively "pure" form of AD as it is almost entirely driven by genetics as opposed to environmental factors. Together, these efforts resulted in the publication of several studies comparing antemortem PET imaging with postmortem neuropathology in autosomal dominant and sporadic AD. Interestingly, our estimate of the typical amount of amyloid-β plaque that accumulates in the brain during AD has, among other things, been used by other investigators to estimate whether removing amyloid-β plaques during anti-amyloid-β antibody treatment could lead to brain shrinkage.

I also took part in some research into anti-amyloid-β antibody therapies through the first clinical trial in dominantly inherited AD (DIAN-TU-001). Again, I compared antemortem PET imaging with postmortem neuropathology, but to deal with the demands of this larger study, I developed an automated method to quantify the levels of amyloid-β deposition, tauopathy, microgliosis, and astrocytosis from hundreds of brain tissue sections. Using this approach, I found that while the anti-amyloid-β therapies we studied do remove some amyloid-β deposits, they didn't seem to alter the levels of tauopathy, microgliosis, and astrocytosis in the brain. In the DIAN-TU-001, I also led or co-led projects investigating the consequences of a major side effect of anti-amyloid-β antibodies (amyloid-releated imaging abnormalities, or ARIA) as well as the feasibility of switching between different amyloid-β PET radioligands for monitoring amyloid-β removal in the event that one is unavailable. Beyond the DIAN-TU-001, I also led a project evaluating the reliability of the first FDA-approved tau-PET radioligand (18F-flortaucipir) for detecting AD tauopathy and found that the manufacturer's guidelines for the visual interpretation of these images did not cover cases such as hemorrhagic infacts and meningiomas, which can imitate the appearance of tau pathology.

Post-baccalaurate research

St. Louis, Missouri | 2015-2018

After college I worked with Dr. Ilya Monosov to study the brain as it learned risks and rewards in an uncertain environment. My thought back then was that studying how biological neurons encoded risks and rewards could inform the design of artificial neural networks. At the time, there was a breakthrough in using neural networks to learn how to play video games without human supervision by using "only the pixels and the game score as inputs". However, when watching videos of the neural network's performance, I noticed it often failed to learn how to play games where the game score increased very rarely. I thought one way to remedy this was to design a cost function that could "motivate" the network to learn how to play in the absence of frequent rewards.

I kept this in mind as I ran experiments to measure a monkey's neuronal activity in the basal forebrain while it watched visual cues that indicated the probability that it would receive a fruit juice reward in the next few seconds. I ended up characterizing two neuronal signals: one was a pre-reward ramping signal that was associated with anticipating a probablistic reward in the future, and the other was a post-reward spiking signal that was associated with receiving a reward that had a low probability of occuring. I felt these signals could be useful in the context mentioned earlier: pre-reward ramping ties together a visual cue and possible future reward, even though they don't occur simultaneously, and post-reward spiking draws the most attention to the rarest rewards, and could be a powerful learning signal in the absence of frequent rewards.

Undergraduate research

St. Louis, Missouri | 2011-2015

In college I worked with Drs. David Van Essen, Matt Glasser, and Krikor Dikranian on a problem they called the "gyral bias". Drs. Van Essen and Glasser were using diffusion MRI to estimate the trajectories of axonal fibers that connect different parts of the brain together. Diffusion MRI suggested that more axonal fibers terminated in gyral crowns than in sulcal banks or fundi. However, Dr. Dikranian found that in postmortem brain tissue that has been stained to visualize axonal fibers, the fibers appeared to terminate evenly along the cortical surface with no preference for gyral crowns. Clearly the diffusion MRI fiber estimates needed to be improved to reflect this, but it was not clear how to quantify the observations from postmortem brain tissue so they can be used to correct these estimates.

First I worked on a way to quantify axonal fiber directions with respect to the cortical surfaces from the postmortem brain tissue. In the beginning, I was given the brain tissue, a microscope, a pencil, and a stack of paper, and I was asked to draw what I saw. This took a lot of time, and I felt that even if I drew the axonal fibers perfectly, they would never be quantitative. I decided to digitize the tissue sections. Then I manually annotated the axonal fibers using Adobe Illustrator, a vector graphics software, which gave a quantitative measure of fiber directions. This still took a lot of time, though, so I taught myself how to code in MATLAB, and wrote a script that estimated axonal fiber directions (using structure tensors) with respect to the cortical surface (delineated using B-splines).

With the ability now to quantify where and by how much axonal fiber directions differed between digital microscopy and diffusion MRI, our findings spun off several new approaches for estimating the trajectories that axonal fibers take when terminating in cerebral cortex. These findings also received pushback from other investigators who believed there was no "gyral bias" and that fiber directions estimated by diffusion MRI always reflected the true fiber architecture of the brain. At the time, using diffusion MRI to estimate axonal fiber trajectories was still a relatively recent development, and I'm sure people were very excited by the possibilities and were happy to overlook the limitations. But now, it's hard to argue that there haven't been substantial improvements to diffusion MRI since then, and that maybe the earlier attempts did indeed have issues like the "gyral bias" that were corrected later.
